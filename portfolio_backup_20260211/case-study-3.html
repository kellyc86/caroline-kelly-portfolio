03 DYNAMIC PRICING INTELLIGENCE

WHAT PRICE MAXIMIZES REVENUE WHILE MAINTAINING SELLOUTS?

This ML model predicts optimal ticket prices based on event characteristics (genre, artist, venue, timing), targeting 85%+ sellout probability while maximizing revenue per event. The model reveals systematic underpricing patterns and provides promoters with data-driven pricing recommendations.

METHOD: RANDOM FOREST REGRESSION

Why Random Forest?

Chosen because:

  • Captures non-linear relationships: Price sensitivity varies by context. Example: "Friday Techno in London commands 40% premium vs Tuesday House in Birmingham" — linear regression would miss these complex interactions.
  • Handles interactions automatically: Doesn't require manual feature engineering for interaction terms (genre × day-of-week × city). Model discovers these patterns from data.
  • Provides feature importance: Reveals which factors matter most for pricing, enabling strategic focus.
  • Robust to outliers: Averaging across trees prevents single extreme events from distorting predictions.

Alternatives Considered

1. Linear regression
Rejected because: Assumes linear price/demand relationship (unrealistic). Example: doubling artist following doesn't simply double sustainable price—relationship is non-linear with diminishing returns.

2. Gradient boosting (XGBoost)
Rejected because: Would provide marginally higher accuracy (~2-3% R² improvement) but at cost of interpretability. Random Forest feature importance is clearer for stakeholder communication. Also, gradient boosting is more prone to overfitting on event data with many categorical features.

3. Manual pricing rules (current state)
Comparison: Manual rules achieve ~R² = 0.72 (promoters use genre + day-of-week heuristics). Random Forest improves to R² = 0.87, capturing additional patterns manual rules miss.

Model Training Details

  • Training data: 15,000 historical events from past 18 months
  • Features engineered:
    • Genre (encoded as dummy variables)
    • Artist social media following (log-transformed to handle skew)
    • Day-of-week (weekend vs weekday)
    • Venue prestige score (derived from historical sellout rates)
    • Competitive pricing (avg price of similar events in same city-week)
    • City population (proxy for market size)
  • Hyperparameters: 100 trees, max depth 10, min samples per leaf 20 (prevents overfitting)
  • Validation: 5-fold cross-validation, R² = 0.87 (consistent across folds)

Critical Limitation: This Is Prediction, Not Causation

Model trained on historical prices, so it predicts "what price was charged" not "what price should be charged."

Risk: If historical pricing was systematically wrong (e.g., promoters chronically underprice due to risk aversion), model perpetuates these errors. It learns patterns from data, including mistakes.

Example of correlation ≠ causation:

  • Model observes: "Weekend Techno events charge £25, weekday House events charge £15"
  • We cannot conclude: "Raising weekday House prices to £25 will maintain demand"
  • Confound: Weekend buyers may have higher willingness-to-pay (different customer segment), not just different timing. Price difference reflects customer differences, not pure pricing power.

To establish causality, we would need: A/B test where we randomly assign prices to similar events and measure sellout rates + revenue. Model provides starting point for experiments, not final answer.

KEY INSIGHTS

Model Performance

R² = 0.87 means model explains 87% of price variance across events. Remaining 13% likely reflects:

  • Promoter-specific strategies: Some promoters always undercut competitors, others premium-price based on curation
  • Unmeasured factors: Artist momentum (viral TikTok moment), social media buzz, recent press coverage
  • True randomness: Pricing decisions aren't always rational (promoter intuition, negotiation dynamics)

Feature Importance: What Drives Pricing?

1. Artist following (32% importance): Dominant factor. Each doubling of follower count associated with ~£3 higher ticket price (non-linear relationship captured by model).

2. Genre (24% importance): Techno and House command 25-30% premium vs other genres, even after controlling for artist size and venue. Reflects both audience willingness-to-pay and production costs (sound systems, lighting).

3. Day-of-week (18% importance): Weekend events charge 35-40% premium vs weekday. Stronger than expected — suggests significant demand segmentation by schedule flexibility.

4. Venue size (15% importance): Larger venues enable higher prices, but relationship isn't 1:1. Doubling capacity only increases price ~18% (diminishing returns to scale).

Systematic Underpricing Identified

Current state: Manual pricing leaves estimated £89K annual revenue on table.

Where underpricing occurs:

  • Weekend Techno with established artists: Currently priced at £22-25, model suggests £28-32 (15-20% increase) would maintain 85%+ sellout rates based on comparable events
  • London events generally: Underpriced by 8-12% vs similar events in Manchester (despite higher local costs and willingness-to-pay)
  • Large venues (500+): Promoters appear risk-averse, pricing conservatively even when demand signals (waitlists, social media engagement) suggest price elasticity

STRATEGIC RECOMMENDATIONS

Staged Rollout: Test, Learn, Scale

Phase 1: Shadow Mode (1 month)

  • Run model alongside current manual pricing
  • Log predictions vs actual prices chosen
  • Compare outcomes: Did model's suggested prices lead to better sellout + revenue?
  • Decision point: If model predictions correlate with successful events (high sellout + high revenue), proceed to Phase 2

Phase 2: A/B Test (2 months)

  • Treatment group (20% of new events): Apply model-recommended prices
  • Control group (80% of new events): Use manual pricing (status quo)
  • Measure: Revenue per event, sellout rate (target >85%), customer complaints about pricing
  • Success criteria: >10% revenue lift in treatment group with no decline in sellout rate or increase in complaints
  • Decision point: If success criteria met, proceed to Phase 3

Phase 3: Full Rollout (ongoing)

  • Make model recommendations default for all new events
  • Allow manual override for high-profile events (reputational risk)
  • Re-train model monthly with new data (capture evolving patterns)

Risk Mitigation Guardrails

  • Price increase caps: Limit model recommendations to max 25% increase vs historical average for that segment (avoids sticker shock)
  • Manual override capability: Promoters retain final pricing control for high-stakes events (preserve relationships)
  • Secondary market monitoring: If model prices too low, tickets resold at premium → signal to increase further. If prices too high, slow ticket velocity → signal to reduce.
  • Feedback loop: Track actual sellout outcomes vs predictions, flag systematic errors for model retraining

Promoter Communication Strategy

Challenge: Promoters may resist algorithmic pricing (threatens their expertise, perceived as "surge pricing" even though it's personalized, not reactive).

Communication approach:

  • Frame as decision support, not replacement: "Model provides starting point, you retain final control"
  • Share feature importance insights: Help promoters understand why model recommends certain prices (educate on drivers)
  • Highlight £89K opportunity: Frame as revenue unlock, not criticism of current pricing
  • Start with willing partners: Pilot with data-savvy promoters first, use their success to convince skeptics

STRATEGIC RATIONALE (PORTER'S FIVE FORCES)

Dynamic pricing reduces buyer power by capturing consumer surplus more efficiently. Instead of single price leaving money on table (high-willingness buyers underpaying) or excluding low-willingness buyers (price too high for price-sensitive segment), model finds optimal equilibrium.

However, excessive price optimization creates risks:

  • Commoditization: If users perceive events as purely price-driven (no differentiation), platform becomes vulnerable to lower-cost competitors. Need to maintain quality/curation signals alongside pricing.
  • Substitute threat increases: Higher prices make free events, house parties, streaming alternatives more attractive. Pricing power has limits.
  • Supplier power (promoter) risk: If model consistently recommends prices promoters perceive as "too aggressive," they may defect to competitor platforms with simpler/lower pricing.

Strategic implication: Use pricing intelligence to capture reasonable premiums in undersupplied segments (weekend Techno with strong artists), but avoid pushing prices to theoretical maximum (preserve customer goodwill and promoter relationships).
